Fifty-Two Keys

Fifty-two yes-or-no questions about the party that holds the White House. A fixed rule answers each one from public data. When more than half of them go that party's way, the keys say it wins the election.

Inspired by Allan Lichtman's 13 Keys to the White House.

Updated November 25, 2026. The figures are checked every hour, and the run ahead is redone whenever one of them changes. The key list is not frozen yet.

Where 2028 stands

Republicans hold the White House, so every key asks whether things are going their way. A key hangs straight when the answer is yes and is knocked aside when the answer is no.

      How it works

      1. Ask fifty-two questions

        Each key is a yes-or-no question about the party in the White House, such as whether unemployment is lower than a year ago. A fixed rule answers it from public figures. Yes means it favors that party.

      2. Score five groups

        The keys fall into five groups: the economy now, the economy this term, approval, party strength, and incumbency and nominations. A group's score is the share of its keys that say yes. The five scores are averaged, so every group counts the same.

      3. Read the result

        If the average is above 50%, the keys say the party in office wins the election. At 50% or below, they say it loses. That is the whole model: the count decides, and nothing is fitted to past results. How far the average sits from 50% says how sure the call is: under 50% or above 70% has been a clear call, and between them the party in office has won about half the time.

      Winning the election means winning the Electoral College, and with it the presidency. The keys make one call each time: the party in office wins, or it loses.

      The party in office won the election It lost

      The four misses are 1952, 2000, 2016 and 2024. Each time the keys said the Democrats, the party in office, would win, and they lost.

      2000 and 2016. The Democrats won the most votes nationwide and lost the Electoral College: by 537 votes in Florida in 2000, after the Supreme Court stopped the recount, and by about 78,000 votes across three states in 2016. The keys read the country as a whole, so they cannot see a split like that coming.

      1952 and 2024. Both times an unpopular president left the race in the election year, Truman in March and Biden in July, and the Democrats nominated someone who had not run in the primaries. Both times a strong economy carried the count just over the line, to 53.9% and 53.2%.

      Why the count is not a forecast of the vote

      The keys call a winner. They were not built to say by how much, and they do it poorly. Below, each past election's share is estimated from a line fitted to the other 18, so no election helps to estimate itself, and set against what the party in office took. In the five elections from 2008 to 2024 a line fitted only on earlier elections missed the margin by 4.3 points on average, each time in the Democrats' favor, and a forecast that used it did worse than the same forecast without it (The Margin's test, linked at the foot of the page). So there is no vote estimate for 2028 here: the count says who is favored, and nothing more exact. Shares are of the two-party vote, for the party in office.

      The ranges in the ranking above are every ten points, round numbers that were not tuned. They describe the record and change nothing in the model: the call is still made at one half.

      The fifty-two keys

      Every key is true when it favors the party in office. A tie counts as false. No cut-off was chosen by looking at election results: each rule compares with zero, a year ago, the start of the term, the previous term, half, or a plain fact.

          The fine print

          What comes from Lichtman

          • The idea. Yes-or-no keys about the party in office, all answered before the election, and a simple count to read them.
          • Six of his thirteen keys are here. Three are now scored by a rule (recession, long-term growth and the midterm mandate), one is a plain fact (a sitting president on the ticket), and two still use his own rating for past elections (third party and nomination contest). Both of those have a rule for 2028.
          • His seven judgment keys are left out: policy change, social unrest, scandal, foreign or military failure and success, and the charisma of each candidate. They cannot be scored by a fixed rule.
          • What is different. The other 46 keys are new, and the keys are read in five equal groups instead of one count.

          The details

          • One call. More than half of the keys in favor, group by group, and the keys say the party in office wins the election. There is no share of the vote and no map.
          • Five groups, equal weight. A group does not count more for having more keys.
          • State economies count through two keys. Each state's index is weighted toward the election, the way Enns and colleagues weight it.
          • No weights, no dropped keys. No key counts more because it did well in the past, and none was removed for doing badly.
          • Nothing chosen by results. Three changes were tried after the results were in and taken out for that reason: a group of their own for the three primary keys, scoring those keys on the eventual nominee, and two keys that could overrule the count. Each one changed only the calls it was meant to fix.

          What to keep in mind

          • Nineteen elections is a small record. One election more or less changes the picture.
          • Four calls were close.
          • The record moves with the keys. A random half of the keys typically calls 14 of 19, and leaving out any one key gives 13 to 15.
          • Two keys still use Lichtman's own ratings for the past (third party and nomination contest). Without them the two closest calls, 1968 and 1988, go the other way and the record is 13 of 19.
          • The figures are today's revised ones, not the first estimates published before each election.
          • The two state keys begin in 1980 and 1984. The state indexes start in 1979, so earlier elections are scored without them.
          • Left out on purpose: head-to-head polls, and anything that needs a judgment call.
          • The run ahead is an estimate, not part of the model. The economy keys are run by laying 117 past stretches of 20 months over today's forecasts, with a recession in 33% of runs. The approval keys take steps the size approval has moved before, and the nomination keys use how often each was yes in the 19 elections here; none of those odds is typed in. Sources: the Philadelphia Fed's Survey of Professional Forecasters (growth, jobs, unemployment, prices, read from each new survey), FRED (the latest figures, read every working day and checked before use; a move too large to pass is checked against the Bureau of Labor Statistics itself), VoteHub's listing of polls of all adults (approval, averaged by pollster) and PollResults.org (polls naming a third candidate). Nothing in the run ahead is typed in; every run is kept in the forecast history.
